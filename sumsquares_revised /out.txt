On the polynomial values represented by quadratic forms
Bogdan Grechuk* Jamal Agbanwa†
July 6, 2026

Abstract
Many Diophantine equations can be reduced to the question of whether, for a given non-degenerate
quadratic form F and a univariate polynomial P with integer coefficients, P( x ) can be represented by F for
infinitely many values of x. We develop a method for answering this question for certain cubic and quartic
polynomials P, as well as for certain polynomials of the form P( x ) = R( Q( x )), where R(t) and Q( x ) are
polynomials of degree 3 and 2, respectively. Applying this method with F (y, z) = y2 + z2 , R(t) = t3 − 4
and Q( x ) = x2 , we conclude that x6 − 4 is a sum of two squares infinitely often. In turn, this implies that
the equation y2 + x3 y + z2 + 1 = 0 has infinitely many integer solutions. Prior to this work, it was the
shortest equation for which it was open whether its integer solution set is finite or infinite. We conclude with
a list of the new shortest equations whose finiteness problem remains open.

Key words: Diophantine equations, integer solutions, quadratic forms, sums of two squares.

Introduction

The study of polynomial Diophantine equations, i.e. polynomial equations with integer coefficients for which
we are only interested in integer solutions, is almost as old as mathematics. Informally, a Diophantine equation
is considered “solved” if we can “describe” all its integer solutions. However, the word “describe” here is
difficult to define rigorously. Instead, one may ask the following weaker but completely formal question.
Problem 1.1. Given a polynomial P( x1 , . . . , xn ) with integer coefficients, determine whether the equation

The length l ( P) is a natural parameter because it approximates the number of symbols needed to write down the
polynomial P as a sum of monomials, provided that all coefficients are written in binary and the power symbol
is not used (e.g., we write x3 y as xxxy). Following [9, Section 1.1.1], we call two polynomial Diophantine
equations equivalent if they can be transformed into one another via renaming or permuting the variables, sign
changes of the variables (xi → − xi ), or multiplication of the whole equation by −1. Then, for any B > 0,
there are only finitely many inequivalent equations (1) satisfying l ≤ B. Hence, it is possible to arrange all
equations (1) in non-decreasing order of their length and systematically solve Problem 1.1 in this order.
In [9, Section 8.3.1], it was reported that Problem 1.1 had been solved for all Diophantine equations of
length l < 9 except (2). It was also remarked that equation (2) can be rewritten as

( x3 + 2y)2 + (2z)2 = x6 − 4.

(3)

Together with an easy parity argument, this implies that Problem 1.1 for equation (2) reduces to the question
whether x6 − 4 can be represented as a sum of two squares for infinitely many integers x.
Problem 1.1 for equation (2) was listed as an open question in the monograph [9], in the collection of open
problems [8], and on MathOverflow1 , where Daniel Loughran presented an advanced proof that x6 − 4 is a sum
of two rational squares for infinitely many rational numbers x. He also remarked that he “would be very happy
to see an elementary answer which avoids all this geometry”.
The sum-of-two-squares question arising from (3) is a special case of a more general problem: when are
the values of a one-variable polynomial represented by a fixed quadratic form? More precisely, if
F (y, z) = Ay2 + Byz + Cz2 ,

A, B, C ∈ Z,

is a binary quadratic form and P( x ) is a polynomial with integer coefficients, one may ask whether
F (y, z) = P( x )

(4)

has infinitely many integral solutions. The classical theory of binary quadratic forms gives a very detailed description of which fixed integers are represented by a fixed form. For y2 + z2 , Fermat’s theorem and its standard
extension characterize the represented positive integers by the parity of the exponents of primes congruent to 3
(mod 4). More generally, the theory of genera, classes, composition, automorphs and local representation is
treated in standard references such as [6, 3, 5, 19, 21]. From an asymptotic point of view, Landau’s theorem on
sums of two squares was generalized by Bernays to primitive positive definite binary quadratic forms [2]; see
also James [17] for the distribution of integers represented by quadratic forms. These results describe the represented integers in the aggregate, but they do not by themselves decide whether a prescribed sparse sequence
P(Z) meets the represented set infinitely often.
When P has degree at most two, equation (4) is itself a quadratic Diophantine equation in three variables. In
principle, such equations are covered by the general algorithm of Grunewald and Segal for integer solutions of
quadratic equations [12]. There is also a direct analytic theorem of Friedlander and Iwaniec [7]: for a quadratic
polynomial P( x ) = ax2 + bx + c and a binary quadratic form satisfying natural local conditions, they obtain
lower bounds of order X (log X )−1/2 for the number of x ⩽ X for which P( x ) is represented. Their proof
uses the half-dimensional sieve [14]. More recently, Cao and Huang obtained geometric-sieve results for affine
quadrics and, as an auxiliary application, density estimates for quadratic-polynomial values represented by
positive definite binary quadratic forms [4]. These results give powerful analytic information in the quadratic
case, whereas the main difficulty of the present paper begins with cubic and higher-degree P.
When P has degree three, the affine surface defined by (4) is closely related to Châtelet surfaces and to
pencils of conics. Iwaniec and Munshi studied rational points on equations of the form Q(s, t) = P( x ), where
Q is an irreducible binary quadratic form and P is an irreducible cubic polynomial [15]. Their result is primarily
about rational points rather than integral points, but it is one of the central analytic works on cubic polynomials
represented by quadratic forms. A recent and more directly integral result is due to Iyer [16], who proves lower
bounds for the frequency with which certain irreducible monic cubic polynomials are sums of two squares; for

There is also an integral-point literature on affine Châtelet surfaces. Gundlach considered integral Brauer–
Manin obstructions for equations y2 + z2 + x k = m, equivalently y2 + z2 = m − x k , and obtained conditional
completeness results under Schinzel’s hypothesis [13]. Berg studied Brauer–Manin obstructions to integral
points on affine surfaces y2 − az2 = P( x ) [1]. These results are close in spirit to the study of Problem 1.1
for equation (4); however, they are primarily local-global statements, and some are conditional on Schinzel’s
hypothesis.
In summary, previous results either solve the degree-⩽ 2 case by the theory of quadratic equations, prove
rational-point theorems or partial integral results in the cubic case, or establish conditional local-global results.
They do not directly provide an elementary construction proving that the degree-six representation problem
Y 2 + Z2 = x6 − 4 has infinitely many integral solutions.
Given that the project of solving Problem 1.1 for all Diophantine equations ordered by length had stalled at
equation (2), recent work has also considered less demanding questions for special families of equations. For
example, if one asks only whether the solution set is non-empty and restricts attention to cubic equations, then
this problem has recently been solved for all cubic equations of length l < 12 + log2 3 ≈ 13.6 [11]. Instead of
restricting the degree, one may restrict the number of variables [9, Section 3], the number of monomials [10],
or consider only equations of Fermat type [20]. See [8, Section 8] for a dynamically updated list of recently
solved equations in various categories.
In this work, we develop a general elementary method for proving, in many cases, that values of a polynomial P( x ) are represented by a given non-degenerate quadratic form F for infinitely many values of x. The
method can be tried if either (i) P is cubic, (ii) P is quartic, or (iii) P is a polynomial of degree 6 representable
as
P( x ) = R( Q( x )),
(5)
where R(t) and Q( x ) are cubic and quadratic polynomials with integer coefficients, respectively. The method is
not guaranteed to work for all polynomials of the forms (i)–(iii), but it works well in many interesting examples.
In particular, applying it to F (y, z) = y2 + z2 , R(t) = t3 − 4 and Q( x ) = x2 , we prove that x6 − 4 is a sum
of two squares infinitely often, and conclude that equation (2) has infinitely many integer solutions.
The resolution of equation (2) completes the project of solving Problem 1.1 for all equations of length
l < 9, and the next step is to study equations of length l = 9. Before this work, there were 24 open equations
of this length; see [8]. Four of them are similar to equation (2), and we resolve these equations by the same
method.
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes a method for proving that values of
certain polynomials of the form (5) are sums of two squares infinitely often. Section 3 applies this method to
solve Problem 1.1 for equation (2), as well as for four equations of length l = 9 that were left open in [9, 8].
Section 4 records an extension of the same tangent construction to general binary quadratic forms. Section 5
summarizes the main consequences and points to some natural next questions.

The sum of two squares case

In this section we study Problem 1.1 for equations of the form
y2 + z2 = P ( x ),
where P( x ) is given by (5). We present the sum of two squares case first, because it is of independent interest, suffices for applications in Section 3, and, most importantly, because the resulting algorithm is easier to
understand and apply.
Let S2 be the set of positive integers representable as sums of two integer squares. Equivalently, [9, Theorem 5.11], S2 is the set of positive integers in whose prime factorizations every prime p with p ≡ 3 (mod 4)
occurs with an even exponent. This characterization immediately implies that
(*) if a, b are positive integers and S2 contains two of the integers a, b, ab, then S2 contains all three of them.
Problem 1.1 for many Diophantine equations reduces to the following problem.
Problem 2.1. Given a polynomial P( x ) with integer coefficients, determine whether there are infinitely many
integers x such that P( x ) ∈ S2 .
If the degree of P( x ) is at most two, the problem reduces to deciding whether the quadratic Diophantine
equation y2 + z2 = P( x ) has infinitely many integer solutions; there is a general algorithm for answering this
question for all quadratic equations [12]. A powerful recipe for solving this problem for cubic polynomials
P( x ) is described in [9, Section 5.2.3]. Here, we present a method that works for many cubic and quartic
polynomials, as well as polynomials of the form (5) with cubic R(t) and quadratic Q( x ). To unify these cases,
we assume that P( x ) has the form (5) with (deg R, deg Q) equal to either (3, 1), or (4, 1), or (3, 2).

Algorithm 2.2.
• Input: Polynomials R(t) and Q( x ) with integer coefficients such that (deg R, deg Q) is equal to either
(3, 1), or (4, 1), or (3, 2).
• Output: If the algorithm terminates, then P( x ) = R( Q( x )) ∈ S2 for infinitely many integers x.
1. By direct search, find u such that R(u) ∈ S2 .
2. For this u, write down equation (9) in the form (10) and check conditions (a), (b) and (c).
3. If all conditions (a)-(c) are satisfied, STOP. Otherwise return to Step 1 and try to find a different u.
This algorithm does not always terminate, because a suitable u is not guaranteed to exist. However, it works
in many interesting examples, as we illustrate in the next section.

multiplicative forms F. The simplest example of a form which is not multiplicative is

Conclusion

We have given an elementary tangent construction for producing infinitely many values of certain polynomials
that are represented by quadratic forms. In the sum-of-two-squares case, the construction reduces the problem
to a Pell-type auxiliary equation. Applied to x6 − 4, it proves that this polynomial is a sum of two squares for
infinitely many integers x. This resolves the finiteness problem for the equation y2 + x3 y + z2 + 1 = 0 and
completes, with respect to Problem 1.1, the classification of all Diophantine equations of length l < 9.
The same method also resolves four of the previously open equations of length l = 9, leaving precisely
the twenty equations displayed in Table 1. Section 4 shows that the construction is not tied to the special norm
form y2 + z2 , nor to multiplicativity: after imposing explicit congruence conditions, the same tangent argument
applies to arbitrary non-degenerate integral binary quadratic forms.
There are two natural directions for further work. The first is to characterize those pairs ( R, Q) and forms F
for which the auxiliary equations produced by the construction have the required integer solutions. The second
is to develop methods for the remaining cases where the resulting auxiliary equations have local obstructions
or otherwise fall outside the Pell-type criteria used here. For example, we do not know whether
y2 + z2 = x 6 + 3
has infinitely many integer solutions. Applying Algorithm 2.2 with R(t) = t3 + 3 and Q( x ) = x2 , any u
satisfying Step 1 must be odd, since even u gives u3 + 3 ≡ 3 (mod 4). For odd u, the auxiliary equation is
4(u3 + 3) x2 − u(u3 − 24) = v2 ,
and its left-hand side is congruent to 3 (mod 4), which is impossible for a square. Thus the present algorithm
cannot resolve this case, and new ideas are needed.
